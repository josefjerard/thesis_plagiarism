Students may cheat for several reasons, and the cause is not always a lack of concern for learning. Some feel intense pressure to earn high grades, keep scholarships, satisfy family expectations, or compete for limited opportunities. Others fall behind because of work, illness, or poor time management and see cheating as a quick way to catch up. A student may also convince themselves that copying is harmless if “everyone does it,” especially when assignments seem disconnected from real learning.

I remember preparing for a quiz after a week when several deadlines collided. I was tempted to rely on a friend’s notes during the test because I worried that I had not studied enough. Instead, I asked the instructor what topics to focus on and used the remaining time to review those areas. That experience showed me how stress can make a bad shortcut seem reasonable, but also how clear guidance can help a student choose differently.

Schools can discourage cheating by combining fair consequences with practical support. Expectations should be explained clearly, including what counts as plagiarism, unauthorized collaboration, or improper use of digital tools. Assignments can be designed to value a student’s reasoning and process, not just a final answer that is easy to copy. Drafts, brief reflections, oral explanations, and projects based on class discussions can make learning more visible.

Prevention also requires attention to the conditions that lead students to cheat. Schools can provide tutoring, writing help, and early academic advising. Teachers can break major assignments into stages and encourage students to seek help before a deadline becomes overwhelming. Assessments should be challenging but reasonable, and students should have ways to discuss workload or personal difficulties without feeling that asking for help is itself a failure.

Consequences still matter because cheating undermines fairness and trust. However, punishment alone may not address the reasons students make that choice. A school is more likely to build academic honesty when students understand its purpose, receive support, and see that honest effort is valued. When students can succeed through legitimate help and clear expectations, cheating becomes less attractive and less necessary.

### p17
An effective teacher does more than explain subject matter. Strong teachers make ideas understandable, create a classroom where students feel safe asking questions, and encourage learners to take responsibility for their progress. Their influence can last because they help students see both the value of knowledge and their own ability to develop.

Clarity is one essential quality. A teacher may know a subject deeply, but students benefit only when that knowledge is communicated in a way they can follow. Effective instructors use examples, explain unfamiliar terms, and check whether the class understands before moving on. They also recognize that students learn at different speeds. Patience and flexibility allow a teacher to explain an idea in another way rather than simply repeat the same instruction.

A memorable teacher also treats students with respect. This does not mean avoiding high standards. In fact, students often work harder when they believe a teacher expects them to improve and will support them in doing so. Fairness matters: expectations should be consistent, feedback should focus on the work, and mistakes should be treated as opportunities to learn rather than reasons for embarrassment.

One instructor I remember noticed that I rarely spoke in class. After a lesson, she asked what I thought about the topic and gave me time to explain without rushing. The next week, I felt more comfortable contributing to a discussion. It was a small interaction, but it changed how I viewed participation. The teacher did not lower expectations; she made it easier for me to meet them.

Good teachers also show curiosity and continue learning. They update their examples, listen to student questions, and acknowledge when a topic has more than one reasonable perspective. This helps students understand that education is not just memorizing information but learning how to examine it.

A teacher’s personality may make lessons enjoyable, but effectiveness depends on more than being entertaining. Preparation, communication, fairness, and genuine care all contribute to a classroom where students can grow. Memorable teachers leave students not only with information, but also with confidence to ask questions, learn from mistakes, and continue exploring subjects after the course ends.

### p18
Too much screen time can be harmful, although the effect depends on what a person is doing, how long they are using a device, and what screen use replaces. Screens support education, communication, entertainment, and work. The problem is not every hour spent with a device; it is excessive or unbalanced use that interferes with sleep, physical activity, concentration, or relationships.

One concern is that long periods of screen use can make it harder to maintain healthy routines. Someone who spends most of the evening watching videos or scrolling may delay sleep, sit for extended periods, and have less time for exercise. Late-night device use can also keep a person mentally engaged when it is time to rest. Over time, poor sleep may affect mood, attention, and academic performance.

I noticed this during a week when I watched short videos between study tasks. I planned to take a five-minute break, but repeated clips stretched the break much longer. I then rushed through my reading and went to bed later than intended. Setting my phone aside while studying helped me finish earlier and feel less distracted. That example showed me how easily screen use can expand beyond the time I planned for it.

Screens can also affect attention when notifications repeatedly interrupt a task. Switching between an assignment and messages may make it more difficult to follow complex ideas. Online interactions can be valuable, but they do not always replace face-to-face conversation. If a person uses screens so much that they withdraw from friends, family, or activities they enjoy, it may be a sign that their habits need to change.

At the same time, strict limits do not fit every person or purpose. Students may need screens for research, assignments, or communication, and digital tools can provide useful access to information. A better approach is to pay attention to how screen time affects daily life. People can schedule breaks, turn off unnecessary alerts, keep devices away during meals, and avoid screens shortly before bedtime.

Too much screen time can be harmful when it crowds out important needs and relationships. By using devices intentionally and protecting time for sleep, movement, and focused work, people can keep technology useful without allowing it to dominate their routines.

### p19
Automation is likely to change work substantially over the next twenty years, but it is unlikely to eliminate the need for human workers altogether. Machines and software can already perform repetitive tasks, process large amounts of information, and assist with routine decisions. As these tools improve, some jobs will shrink or disappear, while new roles will emerge and existing occupations will change.

Jobs built around predictable, repeated tasks may be especially affected. Automated systems can sort products in warehouses, process basic transactions, schedule appointments, or analyze standard documents. This may reduce demand for some forms of routine labor. However, technology often changes individual tasks rather than removing an entire occupation. A worker may spend less time on paperwork and more time helping customers, maintaining equipment, or making decisions that require context.

New jobs may develop in areas such as robotics maintenance, data management, cybersecurity, and the design and evaluation of automated systems. There will also be continued demand for work involving care, teaching, skilled trades, and personal communication. These fields often require judgment, empathy, adaptability, or physical work in changing environments, qualities that are difficult to reduce to fixed instructions.

I saw a small version of this change at a grocery store where self-checkout machines handled simple purchases. The employees were still needed, though; they helped customers resolve errors, manage the machines, and answer questions. The technology changed what staff did during a shift rather than making every human role unnecessary. Larger changes may follow the same pattern, though the transition can be difficult for workers whose tasks are replaced.

The benefits and costs will not be distributed evenly. Businesses may gain efficiency, while workers may face job loss or the need to learn new skills. Governments, schools, and employers can help by providing accessible training, career guidance, and support during transitions. Education should emphasize adaptable skills, including problem-solving, communication, and technical understanding, rather than preparing students for only one narrow task.

Automation will likely create opportunities as well as disruption. The central question is not simply whether machines will take jobs, but whether society will help people move into changing forms of work. With thoughtful planning and fair access to retraining, automation can improve productivity while allowing workers to contribute in roles that benefit from human judgment and creativity.

### p20
Public transportation can improve life in a crowded city by moving many people efficiently without requiring each person to travel in a separate car. Buses, trains, and other shared services can reduce traffic congestion, make essential destinations easier to reach, and create more space for people and public activities. These benefits depend on reliable service, good planning, and access for different communities.

When a transit system is frequent and dependable, residents can travel to work, school, medical appointments, and shops without needing to own a car. This is especially important for people who cannot drive or who cannot afford the costs of a vehicle. A well-connected network can also help employers reach a larger pool of workers and allow students to attend schools or colleges farther from home.

I once took a bus across a busy part of town during rush hour. Cars were moving slowly, but the bus used a dedicated lane for part of the trip and reached its stop sooner than I expected. I could read during the ride instead of concentrating on traffic. That experience made clear how a reliable service can save time and reduce the stress of getting around.

More people using public transit can also mean fewer cars competing for limited road and parking space. This may reduce congestion and lower emissions per passenger, particularly when vehicles are well-used and powered efficiently. Cities can use some of the space otherwise devoted to roads and parking for sidewalks, bike routes, trees, or housing. Less traffic noise and cleaner air can improve daily life for residents.

Transit does not automatically work well. Infrequent routes, overcrowded vehicles, unsafe stops, and poor connections can discourage people from using it. Service may also be limited in outer neighborhoods, leaving residents with few alternatives. City planners should consult the communities affected and invest in accessibility, maintenance, safety, and routes that connect residential areas with workplaces and services.

Public transportation is most effective when it forms part of a broader plan that includes walking and cycling. A city that makes transit affordable, accessible, and dependable gives residents more choices and reduces reliance on private cars. These changes can make crowded urban life less stressful while helping cities use space and resources more wisely.
